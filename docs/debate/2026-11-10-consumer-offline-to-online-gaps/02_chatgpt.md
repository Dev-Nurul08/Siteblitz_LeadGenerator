Principal versus agent

A principal buys ₹55 of scrap and sells it for ₹100. Its revenue is ₹100 and its gross profit before other costs is ₹45.

An agent connects the seller and buyer and earns 20% of the ₹100 transaction. Its revenue is ₹20, and it must still deduct its own expenses.

The ₹100 transaction value is the same, but recognised revenue and business risks are different.

My preference for equipment rentals is provisional. It is the next hypothesis to test, not an endorsement to launch a company.

It addresses a potentially broader consumer need—access to occasionally required physical products without buying them—and allows the founder to start with existing independent suppliers. Whether it can become a national brand remains unproven.

Points still open

Competition: Can independent rental vendors provide a materially better package or price than existing online event providers?

Demand: Will enough households buy the proposed ₹6,000 package at full price?

Supply economics: Is a sustainable 15% commission acceptable to competent independent vendors?

Fulfilment: Can vendors themselves deliver, install, recover and replace equipment without the founder employing a field team?

Risk: Are contracts, customer remedies, insurance and equipment-damage arrangements achievable within the founder's resources?

Acquisition: Can the actual fully loaded customer acquisition cost approach ₹300?

Scale: Does first-city demand and supplier density justify national expansion, or is this just a modest local concierge business?

Alternative markets: Are there narrower, genuinely unserved subsegments in recreational sports, monthly parking or adult hobby sessions that provide stronger repeat demand?

PG meals: Can a compliant, financially attractive independent-kitchen model be established without employed delivery staff?

Founder decision: The founder has not approved any build or launch. All proposed tests remain strategy recommendations.

My strongest conclusion for Round 2: We should not fall in love with any single business category. We should search for an existing offline activity where customers already pay, independent suppliers can profit, the platform earns enough on real transactions, and repeat customers continue to need the platform after discovering the supplier.

That combination—not the absence of an app—is what could eventually justify building a national consumer brand.
